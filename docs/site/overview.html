What is Lighthouse?

A plain-language explanation of what Lighthouse does and why it exists — no technical background required.

The Problem It Solves

Important things happen in public. Companies file disclosures with the SEC. Military aircraft fly near research facilities. Scientists go quiet before a breakthrough. Government press releases change tone before major announcements.

None of these signals is conclusive on its own. But when several of them point at the same organization, at the same time, that combination deserves attention.

Spotting these patterns manually — across SEC filings, aircraft transponder feeds, academic databases, and government websites — would take hours of daily effort. Lighthouse does it automatically.

What a "Signal" Is

A signal is a single piece of observed public information that might be relevant. Examples:

  • A company filed an SEC 8-K disclosing a significant contract.
  • An aircraft with a military callsign flew near a known defense research lab.
  • A researcher who publishes frequently went silent for three months.
  • A DoD press release used unusually urgent language about a technology program.

Each of these is a signal. On its own, it might mean nothing. Signals go stale quickly.

What a "Correlation" Is

A correlation is what happens when Lighthouse finds two or more signals pointing at the same entity within a meaningful time window. That combination is scored and stored as a structured event.

Example: if a military aircraft with a known callsign appears near Lockheed Martin's facility within three days of Lockheed filing an SEC disclosure about a new government contract, that combination becomes a correlation event.

Correlations are the publishable unit. Raw signals are not published.

Why correlations matter more than raw alerts Any individual signal can be coincidental. Two or more independent signals pointing at the same entity at the same time are statistically much harder to dismiss.

What the Graph Is

Every correlation creates a structured record in a graph database. Entities (companies, facilities, researchers) are nodes. Events and signals are connected to them as edges.

This means you can ask questions like:

  • "What signals have touched Lockheed Martin in the last 90 days?"
  • "Which facilities saw military aircraft activity near the same time as SEC filings?"
  • "What other entities are within two hops of MIT Lincoln Laboratory in the correlation graph?"

The graph grows over time. The longer Lighthouse runs, the richer the traversal becomes.

Why Running Over Time Matters

A single observation is a snapshot. A pattern over time is intelligence.

Some of the most meaningful signals are temporal — the same military callsign appearing at a facility on multiple separate occasions, or a researcher returning from silence just before a patent filing burst. These patterns only emerge from accumulated data.

Lighthouse is designed to run continuously on a home server, accumulating observations that a one-time run could never produce.

What Lighthouse Is Not

  • It is not a surveillance tool — it only watches public-domain data sources.
  • It does not access classified information.
  • It does not make conclusions automatically — it surfaces patterns for human review.
  • It does not publish raw alerts — only high-confidence, multi-signal correlation events.
  • It does not use AI in its core logic — the correlation, scoring, and explanation engines are deterministic code.
Honest limitations Lighthouse works with public data that is often incomplete, delayed, or noisy. False positives happen. A military aircraft near a facility is an observation, not proof of anything. Always apply judgment when reviewing findings.

Real Examples of Live Signals

From operational runs in 2026:

  • Callsigns MUSL, PAT16, ZEUS31, OTIS61, ELVIS51 observed near DARPA HQ and Camp Lejeune.
  • SEC material filings from aerospace contractors correlated with ADSB military aviation activity in the same time windows.
  • Government defense lab facility coverage active via targeted mode with facility alias matching.

Who It Is For

Operators

People running Lighthouse on a home server who want to monitor a specific set of targets and review intelligence output.

Developers

Engineers extending the pipeline, adding new signals, or integrating Lighthouse into broader workflows via MCP or API.

AI Agents

The MCP server exposes Lighthouse tools for AI agent consumption — graph queries, alert lookups, and correlation explanations.
